\section{Data}
\owner{Person 1 (2.1, 2.2), Person 2 (2.3), Person 5 (2.4, 2.5)}{1 page}

\subsection{Sources}
There are five public files, all linked by the federal building number (EGID). Three come
from the Canton of Geneva (SITG): the yearly heating index \cite{idc}, the declaration
status of each building \cite{status} and the list of buildings that share a central
boiler \cite{boilers}. Two come from the Federal Statistical Office: the Federal Register
of Buildings and Dwellings (GWR) for the canton of Geneva and its code list \cite{gwr}. All files were downloaded on 13 November 2026.

\subsection{Target and cleaning}
The heating file has one row per building and year. A building's values in
different years are almost the same, so with several years per building the model
could see one year in training and copy the answer for another year in the test set.
We therefore keep one value per building: its latest 3-year average ending in 2023
or later. We use the 3-year average because the legal threshold of
450~MJ/m\textsuperscript{2} per year applies to it \cite{ren}, and we start in 2023
because older averages may describe buildings before renovations or heating changes, while a later start would cost about a third of our houses.


\textbf{Implausible values}: We keep 50--1,500 MJ/m\textsuperscript{2} per year; lower values point to a
wrong floor area, higher ones to data errors (25 buildings removed).

\textbf{Houses definition}: We define houses as buildings with one or two dwellings in the register (GKLAS 1110 and 1121), because the register is the only source that also covers the undeclared houses our model is meant for. This definition covers 95\% of the target group (21,906 of the 23,123 residential buildings with fewer than 5 heat consumers). As a check, 74\% of our houses (1,133 of 1,531) are also listed as houses in the heating file, and the houses on which the two sources disagree have a similar median index (406.5 vs.\ 415).

\textbf{Year effect}: Index values fall over time: for the same buildings, the median fell by 43 between the periods ending in 2023 and 2026, so our older targets may be slightly too high. For houses there is no clear trend. Keeping only periods ending in 2025 would cost us about 450 of our 1,531 houses, so we keep all periods from 2023 on.

\subsection{Features and the dates of the heating information}
\textbf{Features} come only from the building register, the one source that also covers undeclared houses:
construction period, building class, energy source (grouped into gas, oil, district heat, heat pump, wood, electric,
other), heating system, footprint (floor) area, floors, dwellings and municipality. A missing category becomes ``unknown'';
missing numbers are filled inside the pipeline with the median of the training folds only, so no information leaks
from the validation or test data. The heated floor area is never a feature, because a building only has it once it
has declared.

\textbf{Heating changes:} The register shows \emph{today's} heating,
but the target was measured in earlier years. If the heating changed in between, the features describe a different heating than the one that produced the value. We drop two kinds of buildings. (1) 697 buildings (65 houses) whose energy source changed \emph{during} their three target years, so their value mixes two heating systems. Some of these switches look more like a change in how the energy was recorded than a new heating system (among houses, ``other'' $\to$ heat pump 16 times and district heat $\to$ oil 13 times); we drop them anyway to be safe. (2) Among the 14,721 buildings where both files give a real energy group, 928 (100 houses) use a different energy source today than in their target years. Some of these differences are hard to explain as real replacements (e.g.\ 142 buildings from gas to oil, the opposite of the usual direction) and may come from the two files recording the energy source differently. We did not verify this, and it does not change our decision: either way, features and target do not match. The register's update date could not help, because almost all entries carry a date in 2024 or 2025.

\section{Method}
\owner{Person 3 (3.1, 3.2), Person 4 (3.3), Person 5 (3.4)}{1 page}

\subsection{Split and cross-validation}
We split the 14,827 buildings once, with a fixed random seed, and all models use this split.
First, about one seventh of the data (2,117 buildings, 195 of them houses) was set aside as the
test set. It stayed locked until the final evaluation in Section~5 and played no part in choosing
features, models or tuning parameters. The remaining 12,710 buildings (1,171 houses) were split
into five folds of about 2,540 buildings each. In every round of cross-validation, a model is
trained on four folds and judged on the fifth. Two rules shaped the split, both applied with scikit-learn's \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Buildings that share a heating installation get the same declared value. If one of them were
in training and the other in validation, the model could “predict” a building by copying its
twin, and the scores would be too optimistic. We therefore linked buildings on the same
boiler (boiler file) and “twins” with the same reference year, value and heated floor area
into groups: 3,089 buildings (21\%) share a group with at least one other building, the
largest group has 20. Every group lies entirely in one fold or in the test set.
    \item  Second, houses are only 9.2\% of the data
but are the group the office cares about, so every fold and the test set contain the same share of
houses (9.2\%), about 234 per fold. The share of houses above 450~MJ/m\textsuperscript{2} was not
balanced on purpose and varies between 35.9\% and 39.6\% across the parts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Selection measure and baselines}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Selection measure: The office needs to know whose owners to contact first, so we judge models by the list they
produce. In each validation fold we rank the houses by their prediction, take the top 20\%, and
count the share that is really above 450~MJ/m\textsuperscript{2}: the \emph{hit rate}. We report
its mean over the five folds with its standard error (SE), houses first and all buildings next to
them; the same measure also chooses the penalty of ridge, lasso and logistic regression. With
about 47 houses in each top 20\%, one house moves the hit rate by about two points, so differences
smaller than one SE count as chance. As extras we report the hit rate in the top 10\% and the mean
absolute error (MAE) in MJ/m\textsuperscript{2}.
  \item Baselines: every model must beat two baselines, fitted on the training folds like the models.
The \emph{training mean} gives every building the same value, so its list is random: its hit rate is
about the share of houses above 450 (38.0\% in cross-validation). The \emph{expert rule} gives each
building the mean index of its construction period $\times$ energy group in the training folds
(an unseen combination gets the overall mean). This is what an energy expert could do without a
statistical model, and the bar a model has to clear to be worth using.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extra check: a tree model}
 To see whether the register holds more information than a linear model can use, we also fitted gradient
boosting (\texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor}). It builds many small decision trees one after
another, each correcting the errors of the previous ones, so it can find interactions such as
``old, oil-heated and small'' by itself. It uses the same pipeline, folds and selection measure as
the other models. The tree size (7, 15 or 31 leaves) was chosen like $\alpha$; the learning rate was
fixed at 0.05 with 300 trees, and early stopping was switched off, because it would make the result
depend on a random hold-out.

Trained on all buildings, gradient boosting reaches the highest hit rate on houses of all variants,
61.7\%~$\pm$~4.4 (ridge on all buildings: 54.5\%), and the lowest MAE (102.2 on houses, 72.2 on all
buildings). Trained on houses only, it reaches 59.1\%~$\pm$~4.1. Compared fold by fold with ridge
trained on houses only (60.4\%), the difference is small and uncertain: $+1.3 \pm 2.9$ and
$-1.3 \pm 3.1$ points. A flexible model therefore ranks houses about as well as a linear model that
learns from houses only. Most of the gain over the linear models trained on all buildings seems to
come from treating houses differently from other buildings, which the tree model does by itself and
ridge does by learning from houses only.

\subsection{Trained on houses only versus all buildings}
Only about 9\% of our buildings are houses (1366 houses out of 14827 buildings). The question is: does a model that learns only from houses rank them better? Version A learns from all buildings of the four training folds (about 10165--10169), version B only from their houses (about 936--937). Both are judged on the same validation houses. For ridge, each version gets its own alpha because the best amount of shrinkage depends on how much data the model learns from, so we tune each one separately in cross-validation to compare both at their best setting.

\midrule

                  & B: houses only   & 57.9 & 108.3 & 3 of 5 folds \\

Learning from houses only puts more problem houses on the list: When the model learns only from houses, about 57.9--60 of every 100 contacted owners really are above 450, compared with about 54--54.5 when it learns from all buildings(Table~\ref{tab:housesonly}). The evidence is moderate. For linear regression the gain is small and could be chance: $+3.8$ points on average ($\pm 3.4$ standard error), better in only 3 of 5 folds. For ridge it is more convincing:
$+6.0$ points ($\pm 2.4$ standard error), better in 4 of 5 folds. In the top 10\% the gap is even larger (ridge 54.4\% vs.\ 43.1\%, linear regression 58.8\% vs.\ 49.2\%), but these numbers are less reliable: each validation fold has about 234 houses, so the top 10\% holds only about 23 of them. The MAE gets slightly worse, so learning from houses only does not predict the exact values better, but it puts the houses in a
better order, which is what matters for the list. Houses seem to be different enough from
other buildings to justify their own model, even though it learns from about 11 times fewer
rows. One caveat: alpha was chosen on the same folds, so the ridge result is slightly too
optimistic. The final choice is made in Section~5.1.

\subsection{Error analysis}
Where does the model go wrong? We look at its cross-validation predictions, where each
house is predicted by a model that never saw it, and ask three questions.

\textbf{How far off are the predictions?} The model plays it safe: its predictions spread only about half as much as the true values (standard deviation 72 vs.\ 152). A house with a true index up to 250 is predicted on average 171.6 too high, and a house above 700 on average 324.9 too low. Near the threshold the errors are much smaller. This is why the average error is large (MAE 107.1 MJ/m\textsuperscript{2} per year). For the office, this has two consequences. First, the predicted index of a single house is not reliable, especially for very good or very bad houses. Second, the ranking still works, because a house with a high true value usually still gets a higher prediction than a house with a low true value. The model is poor at saying \emph{how high} a value is, but good enough at saying \emph{which house comes first}, and the list only needs the second.

\textbf{Which houses end up on the list?} The office would contact the 20\% of houses with the highest predictions (235 of 1,171 houses). A house on this list is a \emph{hit} if it is really above 450, and a \emph{false alarm} if not: 142 are hits and 93 are false alarms. Two features dominate the list. Oil-heated houses make up only a third of all houses (32.5\%), but 70.4\% of the hits and 79.6\% of the false alarms. Houses built
between 1946 and 1980 make up 65.5\% of the hits and 75.3\% of the false alarms. The problem houses that the list misses look different: 68.0\% heat with gas, and about a fifth (20.8\%) were built before 1919. Their median prediction is 430, just below the threshold of 450, so the model sees them as ordinary houses. Many false alarms are close calls: 59 of the 93 have a true value between 400 and 450.

\textbf{Why are some errors so large?} The biggest errors come from information the register does not have. Some small houses have extremely high values, possibly because of a wrong floor area in the heating file. Some old houses have very low values, probably because they were renovated. The register records neither, so no model built on it can predict these houses well.
